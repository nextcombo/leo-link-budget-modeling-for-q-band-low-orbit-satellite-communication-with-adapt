# [LEO] Link budget modeling for Q-band low orbit satellite communication with adaptive coding and modulation

1. Abstract: This paper proposes a modeling and evaluation framework for Q-band low Earth orbit (LEO) satellite links, integrating time-varying propagation characteristics, real noise temperature modeling, and adaptive coding modulation (ACM) technology. The simulation of the satellite transit process is conducted at 1Hz frequency, matched with meteorological observation data from the same location (resampled from 10 minute resolution to 1 second resolution).
The impacts of atmospheric absorption, cloud cover, and rainfall are integrated into an additional path loss (EPL) term, which serves as input for calculating sky brightness and system noise temperature for both uplink and downlink. Subsequently, the link budget generates a time series of carrier-to-noise ratio spectral density (CNR), driven by ACM selector operation based on the Shannon's Theorem threshold (including implementation margin and lag characteristics).
This paper presents two contrasting case studies: one is a stable transit scenario where both uplink and downlink maintain the highest rate of modulation coding scheme (approximately 947 Mbit/s); the other is a severe fading transit scenario where deep fading leads to frequent switching of modulation coding schemes, resulting in continuous decrease in throughput. The results show that: (1) coupling between sidelobes and atmospheric radiation significantly increases the downlink noise temperature; (2) upward link energy leakage to cold space reduces the effective temperature of the satellite receiver; (3) a moderate lag characteristic can avoid mode oscillation without sacrificing throughput. The framework supports parameter scanning and incorporates measured hardware parameters to match actual operational systems, providing a practical foundation for dynamic link assessment and switching strategy design.
II. Introduction Satellite communication (SATCOM) systems are key supports for global connectivity, providing services such as broadband internet, telecommunications, earth observation, and navigation [1][2]. Link budget is the core of satellite communication system design and optimization, covering all gains and losses experienced by signals from the transmitter to the receiver during transmission [2][3]. The link budget not only determines whether the communication link can meet the quality of service (QoS) requirements but also guides the selection of antenna size, transmit power level, and system margin [2][3].
The rapid deployment of large-scale low Earth orbit (LEO) satellite constellations brings new opportunities and challenges to link budget analysis. Unlike geosynchronous satellites that are relatively stationary on the surface of the Earth, LEO satellites operate at lower altitudes and move faster in the sky, leading to frequent satellite switching, variable propagation distances, and changing elevation angles. Consequently, free space path loss, atmospheric absorption, and weather-related attenuation exhibit dynamic characteristics. In higher frequency bands, such as, these changes are more significant; while in Q and V bands, rainfall, cloud cover, and gaseous attenuation can severely affect link performance, and industry attention to these two bands is continuously increasing [4]-[7].
Traditional link budget calculations often assume static conditions or rely on simplified atmospheric models, which may underestimate environmental influences. Moreover, affected by climate change, the intensity and frequency of extreme weather events are increasing, further magnifying the limitations of traditional methods [8]. To support next-generation satellite systems, more accurate and adaptive methods are needed. Integrating real measured or predicted algorithm-verified real atmospheric attenuation models into the link budget framework can enhance the reliability of performance assessment and provide a more comprehensive basis for network design decisions.
(I) Relevant Work
In the preliminary research, the research team developed a framework for simulating the orbital trajectories of five low-orbit satellites on March 29, 2025. The selection of this date was to align with the meteorological data measured at ground stations - the meteorological station is approximately 1.77 kilometers away from the simulation ground station, located at 38.23444°N and 21.74333°E, which is nearly the same altitude as the simulation ground station (38.21868°N and 21.74641°E). This operation is co-operated by Mr. Giorgos Chronopoulos with the Athens National Observatory [10][11].
The study used Kepler orbit parameters listed in Appendix C to generate satellite trajectories at a resolution of 1 second, providing detailed position data throughout the day. At the same time, a ground station model was built to establish a link with the satellite, observing 19 visible transits. To simplify analysis, the antennae of the ground station and the satellite were not explicitly modeled, assuming that the equivalent isotropic radiated power (EIRP) remained at its maximum gain state during each transit.
To match the trajectory resolution, the study employed a Gamma autoregressive distribution, interpolating the original 10-minute interval rainfall rate data to 1-second intervals. Based on this dataset, atmospheric absorption was calculated, and the rain-induced attenuation was modeled as a Gaussian random process (statistically accounting for cloud layer attenuation). These components were integrated to obtain the total extra path loss (EPL) as a function of angle and frequency. Subsequently, a Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) neural network was trained using EPL data from 18 satellite transits, with the data from the 19th transit used for model testing. The results showed that the model had high prediction accuracy for EPL.
(II) Research Contributions and Scope
This study builds on previous work by utilizing the same satellite overpass data and measured meteorological data to integrate the EPL (Earth-to-Orbit Link Performance) into a comprehensive link budget assessment. This method enables the evaluation of link performance under real-time time-varying propagation conditions, considering both the impacts of uplink and downlink. Additionally, this research supports dynamic system parameter assessments and employs adaptive coding and modulation schemes to maintain reliability performance as channel conditions change.
The structure of the document is as follows: Part II introduces the basic principles of link budget analysis; Part III elaborates on interpolating measured meteorological data to match the resolution of EPL calculations with satellite orbit trajectories; Part IV discusses the modeling methods for total system noise, including contributions from internal electronic device noise, antenna noise, and propagation channel noise; Part V outlines the implementation of adaptive coding and modulation based on channel characteristics; Part VI presents ACM (Adaptive Coding and Modulation) performance results under two satellite overpass scenarios.
Conclusion: This study proposes a comprehensive framework for link budget modeling in low-Earth-orbit Q-band satellite communication systems, selecting appropriate modulation and coding rates based on link performance. The framework incorporates both internal thermal noise generated by electronic components and atmospheric thermal noise encountered during signal propagation through both uplink and downlink channels.
These factors are integrated into the adaptive coding and modulation (ACM) algorithm based on the Shannon capacity theorem, enabling dynamic selection of modulation and coding schemes according to real-time channel conditions throughout the satellite overpass process. In addition to performance evaluation, this framework also provides a foundation for practical applications such as satellite constellation management, aiding in the elimination of unsuitable candidate satellites and determining optimal switching satellites.
Future research could further expand this method by incorporating measured hardware parameters, advanced channel models, and standardized communication protocols, making it more aligned with actual operational scenarios.
